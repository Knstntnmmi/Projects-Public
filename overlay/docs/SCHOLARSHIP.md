# What the scholarship says, and what this project takes from it

This page sets down what a small body of published scholarship states on points that touch this project, and what the project does with each point. The scholarship is mostly by Michael Lackner and by scholars who took part in the Erlangen consortium "Fate, Freedom and Prognostication" (IKGF). Every work is listed in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md), with how far it was read.

In short: the page shows what scholars say BaZi and the other arts are, how they were and are used, and how they speak of uncertainty. It shows what the project takes from each point, which is little and is named. It shows what could not be read.

How to read it. Each theme gives what a scholar states, with author, title, series, year and printed page, and then one sentence that says what the project takes from it or does not take. "Writes" marks a scholar's own text. "Reports" marks a scholar's summary of other people's work. Interviews, newsletter reports, announcements and short project descriptions are spoken or reported statements, or statements of plans, and are labelled so. Where the project draws a conclusion of its own, the text says "the project infers". A quotation is at most 25 words and each source is quoted at most twice; everything else is paraphrase with a page number. The IKGF Occasional Papers carry no series number in the files read, so they are cited by title and year. "fate" is the IKGF newsletter, cited by issue number and cover date.

Nothing here is a rule, a topic definition, a convention or a computation of this project. The last two sections say what these readings do not settle and what could not be read. The terms of art that the forecast prints are defined in [GLOSSARY.md](GLOSSARY.md).

## 1. What kind of art BaZi is

- Michael Lackner writes, in "The Renaissance of Divinatory Techniques in the People's Republic - a New Type of Chinese Cultural Identity?" (IKGF Occasional Papers, 2011), p. 4, that the attribution of qualities to points in time, in the manner of an almanac, led by the twelfth century in Western reckoning to the Chinese horoscope called bazi suanming, which he renders as calculation of fate from the Eight Characters. He names no source for the date. He adds that "the term chronomancy, meaning divination based on time attributes" is more accurate than Chinese astrology, because stellar constellations play a quite subordinated role. Elsewhere he renders the basis of the art as the Eight Cyclical Signs (pp. 24 and 38).
- Marta Hanson, in her signed report on a panel of the ICHSEA (International Conference on the History of Science in East Asia) conference held in Paris in July 2015 (fate no. 10, 04|16, p. 21), defines chronomancy as "all divination techniques that take temporal parameters as their primary variables". She counts finger reckoning of fate (dactylomancy) as a bodily form of it.
- Sophia Katz reports on a study day of 4 July 2012 (fate no. 6, 04|13, pp. 23-24) that Lackner's paper, using Yuan Shushan's biographies of diviners, examined the uses of observation (guan) in physiognomy, palm reading and the Four Pillars of Destiny (sizhu), also known as bazi suanming.
- The newsletter report on the conference "Mantic Arts in China" of July 2016 (fate no. 12, cover date 09|17, pp. 28-29) says that Goldin held it anachronistic to call certain ancient Chinese forecasting methods mantic, because the word comes from a Greek term that the report glosses as mad or raving. It says that divination is not ideal either, since the etymology suggests an intervention of deities, which is not always the case in Chinese methods, and that "there is no perfect word for the predictive techniques we study". It adds that it may be unwise to lay too much emphasis on etymology, provided that scholars are specific about what they mean by such terms.

What the project takes from it: the project keeps the name BaZi for the chart it computes, uses chronomancy in the glossary for arts that take the time of an event as their main input, and defines mantic, divination and prognostication where it uses them; it takes no boundary, table or rule of the BaZi chart from these works, because none of them gives a rule for the pillars, the Zi hour, the turn of the year at Lichun or the luck pillars.

## 2. The texts and the history of BaZi

- Lackner writes (2011, p. 24) that Wan Minying's *Sanming tonghui*, of about 1550, is the standard work for calculating destiny from the Eight Cyclical Signs, and that it appeared in 2009 in an illustrated edition in a series of illustrated classics. He calls Yuan Shushan (p. 7) one of the most knowledgeable twentieth-century authorities on divination, and describes his *Mingpu*, published as late as the 1940s, as biographies of 64 famous Chinese, starting with Confucius, all based on their horoscopes. In the interview with Luo Ying (spoken; the year is not printed) he gives the number as 62. The two counts are not reconciled here.
- Lackner, interviewed by Huang Xiaofeng for The Paper on 12 October 2014 (spoken, no source given), says: 算命从唐代就有了萌芽，宋代开始得到普及. That is, fate calculation began to sprout in the Tang and spread in the Song, and the Ziping method appeared in the Song. In the same interview he says that Chinese reckoning of a person's fate goes by the time of birth and does not look at the sky.
- Manfred Kubny's project page at the IKGF (a fellow's short description; research stay January to September 2011) describes the *Ditian sui* as a classic of bazi suanming attributed to Liu Ji (1311-1375), with commentaries by Chen Su'an (late Ming) and Ren Tieqiao (published after 1820; the *Chan wei*). It says that the *Chan wei* includes 512 explained example charts and names yongshen, xishen, jishen and xianshen as its main concepts.
- Guggenmos's lecture on the spread of prognostic techniques in present-day Taiwan, as abstracted in fate no. 1 (06|10, p. 6, in German), reports from the Taiwan Social Change Survey (1994, 1999, 2004) that about a quarter of the population had used a prognostic technique at least once, that bazi suanming was by far the most consulted, and that most people believed in the techniques only a little. The survey covers Taiwan only.

What the project does not take from it: any date, rule or convention of BaZi, because these are a scholar's summary statements and leads to primary texts (the *Sanming tonghui*, the *Mingpu*, the *Ditian sui* and its commentaries), and the project's BaZi rules rest on the primary texts named in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

## 3. The words of fate

- The IKGF research outline (English, undated, a statement of plans), p. 5, says the first task is to examine the terms used for fate, ming, yun, xing, fen and yuan and their combinations, and to ask how human nature (xing) and destiny (ming) are held to depend on each other. On p. 6 it says that, following Fu Sinian (1952), the senses of ming are to be told apart as fate, moral calling, lifespan and calling or mandate from Heaven.
- Marc Kalinowski writes, in "Destin et divination chez Wang Chong (27-env. 97)" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 2009), p. 6, that Wang Chong holds there are two destinies, both conferred by Heaven at birth: a vital destiny, which fixes bodily form and length of life, and a social destiny, which fixes honour and dishonour, wealth and poverty. Kalinowski writes that destiny is for Wang Chong only a general tendency, which Wang Chong identifies with the divinatory categories of the auspicious (ji) and the inauspicious (xiong). On pp. 8-9 he writes that the destiny given at birth unfolds through circumstances (shi), encounters (yu), coincidence (ou), luck (xing) and fortune (lu), so that a person of auspicious destiny may miss the expected glory if born in a period of decline. On p. 22 he calls Wang Chong's determinism much attenuated for that reason.
- Richard J. Smith, "Key Concepts of Fate and Prediction in the Yijing (Classic of Changes)" (IKGF Selected Articles, 25 June 2011, revised 18 August 2011), pp. 1-2, 5 and 22, says in the abstract that the paper gives particular attention to the terms zhiming (knowing fate) and liming (establishing fate) and to the evaluative vocabulary of the *Changes* (ji, xiong, hui, lin, jiu and wujiu, li, zhen, heng, yuan). On p. 22 he glosses the *Changes* as letting humans know fate and also do something about it, that is, establish it. The title page asks that the paper not be quoted, so it is paraphrased here. Smith counts about 130 occurrences of negative terms (trouble, regret, distress, threatening, ominous) and about 430 of positive terms (auspicious, favourable, advantageous, successful) in the basic text (p. 11), and writes that over the centuries heng, zhen, fu, hui and lin were reread in moral terms (p. 13).
- Hsien-huei Liao, "Readings in Zhu Xi and his Concept of Fate" (IKGF Occasional Papers, reading session of 12 May 2010), reports on two texts of 1169. In a preface, as Liao translates it, Zhu Xi treats human fortunes as immutable; in an anecdote written decades later he tells a disciple that fortune changes with a person's actions (pp. 2-3). Liao says it is open whether the Song literati drew a clear line between fate and fortune (p. 6), and that the seminar reached no consensus (p. 8).
- Vincent Durand-Dastès, "Divination and Fate Manipulation in a Popular Myth of Late Imperial China" (IKGF Occasional Papers, reading session of 11 November 2010), p. 32, quotes Robert Campany, writing about medieval China: the whole point of ming was its ineluctability, and yet many esoteric and Daoist practices aimed to alter or circumvent it. On p. 30 he writes that in a novel of the 1848 edition the diviner incarnates an order of fate that can be read and cannot be changed.

What the project does not take from it: any definition of a word the forecast prints, because the four words supports, cautions, mixed and nothing said are the project's own and do not translate ji, xiong or any other term of these works.

## 4. Words that carry a verdict

- Iwo Amelung, in the abstract of his lecture of 3 July 2012 (fate no. 6, 04|13, pp. 6-7), reports that the term mixin ("superstition") entered Chinese in 1899, perhaps coined by Liang Qichao, and that the relation between superstition and science was highly ambiguous; in the People's Republic the boundaries between science, pseudo-science and superstition were often blurred.
- Lackner writes (2011, pp. 7-8) that campaigns against "superstition" in 1928 and 1930 banned practitioners from their profession, that the literature could still be published, and that the prohibitions were anything but easy to apply or uphold.
- In a spoken interview (Liaowang Dongfang Zhoukan, 22 August 2015; no byline on the copy read) Lackner says that divination as a whole was never declared "superstition" and banned in China, though single arts were sometimes declared secret. He says that "superstition" (mixin) is a word from outside China, whose Western concept passed through Christianity and the Enlightenment and became a term of criticism, and that three masters of the arts of fate (mingli), Xu Lewu (徐乐吾), Yuan Shushan and Wei Qianli, appeared in the 1940s. The text does not say that they were masters of BaZi.
- In the interview with Luo Ying (spoken) Lackner says that he does not fully believe these arts, and that he is interested in their way of thinking, their internal logic and their internal rationality. The sentence is 我自己并不完全相信这些.
- The announcement of his lecture at Sichuan University of 19 June 2021, as reposted on 18 June 2021 (an announcement, authorship of the abstract not stated), says that belief or disbelief is not the concern of the lecture. The workshop announcement in fate no. 14 (12|18, p. 15) says that the mantic arts have long been dismissed as superstitious or irrational, and that this "rejected knowledge" sheds light on the history of science and technology.

What the project takes from it: the project does not use "superstition" as a label for any technique, and says of every statement only what a named rule says.

## 5. Tendencies and stated uncertainty

- Lackner writes, in his editorial of fate no. 15 (06|19), p. 3, in a report on a conference held at the Leopoldina in October 2018: "Predictions indicate tendencies and thus undermine a strictly deterministic view of the world". The sentence follows his summary of two contributions, and the text does not say whose view it is. He adds that predictions give the questioner the opportunity to negotiate with the future or with fate already in the present, and names examples from Tibet, China and Africa. On p. 4 he reports of the closing discussion that "only a communication that relentlessly addresses the uncertainties involved in any prediction" can start a rational reception of it.
- In the Liaowang interview (spoken), Lackner says that in a civilisation that believes in the prediction of fate, knowledge gained from divination allows one, in theory, to negotiate with fate and so to be free. The same interview lists the ways of dealing with fate: magical arts, morality and self-cultivation, offerings to please the ancestors, following the day-book, Buddhist remedies.
- Kalinowski writes (2009, pp. 6, 8-9 and 22) that for Wang Chong destiny is a general tendency whose realisation depends on circumstances (see section 3).
- Smith, in the 2011 paper, pp. 41-42, reports that Wang Bi read a hexagram as marking a situation with room to act between what is fated and what can be done.
- Walter Sparn, "Rise and Fall of Prognostic Astrology in Scientific Paradigms of Early Modern Protestantism" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 17 December 2013), pp. 16-17, reports that Trew, a professor at Altdorf in the seventeenth century, kept the personal horoscope as something that finds characteristic tendencies without necessitating them. Sparn calls the horoscope that finds "tendencies" but does not necessitate "again an axiom of Melanchthon and earlier of Thomas of Aquin" (p. 16). He does not say it of Lilly.
- Elena Esposito, "A Time of Divination and a Time of Risk: Social Preconditions for Prophecy and Prediction" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 30 November 2011), p. 6, writes that predicting a future linked with destiny is completely plausible if one thinks of a future that pre-exists, and, on p. 17, that the future is open and can be neither controlled nor predicted by the models of risk management.

What the project takes from it: the project infers from these characterisations that its statements are best given as what a named rule says about a period, with health statements marked as a tendency only and each list of statements saying that they have not been tested; the choice is the project's own and is not a rule of any tradition.

## 6. How the traditions dealt with checking, and why the scorecard is the project's own

- Lackner writes, in the editorial of fate no. 18 (07|21), p. 3, asking what happens to a forecaster whose forecast fails to materialise: "The answer is initially: usually nothing." He gives two reasons: the forecast may have been cryptic or ambiguous, or "forecasters may admit formal errors, but rarely question the core of their statement". On p. 4 he writes that modesty is not a virtue of expertise. He is writing about forecasting in general and including the forecasts of the present. He makes no claim that any tradition lacked a way of checking.
- Esposito (2011), p. 5, writes: "Should reality falsify them, then it is due to a flawed interpretation, not a false omen." The sentence describes the logic of divination as she sees it. It is not a statement of practitioners and not about BaZi or astrology.
- Daniel Burton-Rose, "Spirit-Altar Prophecy and the Civil Service Examinations in Qing Dynasty China: The Pengs of Suzhou", in Schäfer, Zhao and Lackner (eds), *Accounting for Uncertainty* (Preprint 496 of the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science, Berlin, MPIWG, 2019), p. 50, writes that the way to show that an advocated path to certainty worked was to show that it had worked, and that mid- and late-Qing morality books therefore carried many anecdotes. One book of 1880 had 195 and another of 1899 had 163. The compiler of the first called them "cases of proof", and Burton-Rose says they served an evidential function. The text does not say whether stories of failure appear.
- Liao (2010), p. 2, reports that Zhu Xi's respect for the diviner Xu rested on Xu's command of yin-yang and the five phases and on his accurate forecasts of who in the locality would pass the jinshi examination and of the fortunes of several of Zhu Xi's acquaintances. The circle is not named, and no record, count or scoring is described.
- Zhou Zhenhe's IKGF project page on verification (yingyan) in the Records of the Five Phases of the standard histories (a fellow's short description) says that the terms verification and prognostication are contrary but complementary, and that the more events are corroborated, the more accurate the verifications and the more authoritative the prognostications.
- Ulrike Ludwig, "Magical Choices. Geomancy as a Strategy in the Politics and Everyday Life of Augustus, Elector of Saxony (1526-1586)" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 11 November 2014), writes (p. 13) that in the Dresden records "usually only the question, the geomantic figure and the answer were recorded". She describes the checking as running from geomancy to the world, since geomancy was used to check incoming news (p. 16), and does not describe an answer later compared with an event. One consequence for an official after an unfavourable answer is recorded (p. 21).
- Alexander Fidora's lecture abstract of 10 July 2012 (fate no. 6, 04|13, p. 7) reports that in the fourteenth century medicine and meteorology developed a conditional model that allowed a prognosis to be falsified or revised, whereas "the idea of a global prediction, which is unaffected by the course of events, remained decisive for the mantic arts".
- Johannes Fried, "'Ich dachte, die Welt ginge unter.' Säkularisation als Bewältigungsstrategie" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 2009), p. 12, writes that when a forecast failed, improved calculations were meant to correct the obsolete ones and to confirm the forecast as a whole. Sparn (2013), p. 17, reports one sentence of Trew's: that astrology, so often falsified and abused for fraud, would no longer be trusted. Ulrich Lau's comparison of nearly 50 dates in Qin and early Han criminal case records (fate no. 10, 04|16, p. 10) found that the day-book prescriptions were not followed by common people or officials in those cases; that is about how far prescriptions were used, not about how accurate they were.

What the project takes from it: the scorecard, a record of events that a person enters and compares with frozen forecasts, is this project's own addition and no tradition's test, and the project does not present any record described above as a model for it.

## 7. Comparing practices before worldviews

- Lackner writes, in the programme text of fate no. 1 (06|10, in German, pp. 1-2), that the guiding hypothesis of the consortium is that a civilisation's attitude to the availability and validity of prognosis determines its attitude to the future, and that comparison is to be systematic by topic and not merely additive.
- Lackner writes, in the editorial of fate no. 4 (01|12), p. 2, that besides the comparisons made so far from a bird's-eye perspective, comparisons based on concrete transcultural encounters are to provide fresh information about different attitudes to fate, freedom and prognostication. In the editorial of fate no. 9 (09|15), p. 2, he calls Vernant's 1974 collection, apart from its introduction, "essentially additive".
- Lackner writes, in the editorial of the issue 05|14 (printed p. 2), that studying practices should "shed more light on those worldviews that formed the basis of mantic arts", and not the reverse. He says that worldviews and cosmologies may stand behind the practices but that for the time being they are of secondary importance, and that comparison cannot succeed by looking for conceptual analogies alone.
- An English abstract on a Fudan announcement page dated 25 September 2014 (the page does not make clear which lecture it belongs to, and does not name the author of the abstract) names the axes of comparison between China and Europe as oral against written oracle, inspiration against calculation, and the elite's rating of divination in different periods, and calls the talk a preliminary overview.
- In the Liaowang interview (spoken, as rendered by a journalist), Lackner says that Western natal astrology can be compared to Chinese bazi suanming (西方的生辰星占学可比之于中国的八字算命) and that Bonatti's statement that astrology can say when to begin building is comparable to the Chinese art of choosing auspicious times. He says that both calculation and prophecy exist in both cultures, with different emphasis. He calls the explanation that Chinese prediction works because heaven and man are one a view with something in it but too simple, and says that both star arts rest on the resonance of heaven and man but arise from different cultural contexts.
- Lackner writes, in the note on the planned exhibition in fate no. 17 (08|20), p. 4, that not all the methods can be "matched" appropriately (feng shui is unknown in Europe and there are few traces of Ptolemaic astrology in China) but that "the parallels in terms of both systematic elaboration and interpretation are simply overwhelming".
- Lackner writes, in an article of 2003 in *Ershiyi shiji* no. 76 (April 2003), p. 86, that comparison usually starts from a few features reduced to main characteristics, that the comparer assumes neutrality, and that comparisons are rarely free of interests.
- Smith, "Jesuit Interpretations of the Yijing (Classic of Changes) in Historical and Comparative Perspective" (IKGF Occasional Papers, conference paper of 13 October 2001), p. 56, reports that Chinese commentators had for two thousand years charged one another with "forcing a fit" (qiangpei) between symbol and meaning. In the 2011 paper, pp. 14, 40-41, 44 and 56-58, Smith reports critics of correlations between systems that do not share units: Wang Bi set aside the numerical, astrological and calendrical calculations of the Han tradition, Lu Cai called the matching of unlike numbers a forced fit, and Huang Zongxi rejected the images that later scholars added, as false.
- Esposito (2011), p. 4, writes that in the logic of divination all objects and events reflect the same logic and correspond to each other, so that signs from different domains refer to one another. She does not discuss combining systems.

What the project takes from it: the project sets techniques side by side by what each counts and does, and not by a shared cosmology, and its grades of correspondence (exact, structural, historical, loose, coincidence) are the project's own inference and are not Lackner's.

## 8. Computation in divinatory arts

- Stéphanie Homola, "Reducing Uncertainty through Computation in Chinese Divinatory Arts", in *Accounting for Uncertainty* (MPIWG Preprint 496, 2019), pp. 55-68, reports from primary sources, contemporary manuals and fieldwork. In her words, "Systemized forms of divination usually follow a three steps process" (p. 56): input is encoded into numerals, the numerals are computed, and the result is associated with mantic figures. She treats only the second step.
- Homola reports (pp. 55-56, 58-59, 67) that chronomancy takes dates as input, either of the divination or of the birth, and counts them out, usually on the hand, in a cycle of six terms (Small six ren) or twelve terms (Treasure of the palm). She reports (p. 57) that what matters is the remainder of a division and not the quotient, and that the remainder points into a finite list of figures (eight trigrams, 64 hexagrams, six or twelve positions).
- Homola reports from an interview of 2010 with an amateur diviner in Kaifeng a plum-blossom method (pp. 67-68): year, month and day are added and divided by 8 for the upper trigram, year, month, day and hour are added and divided by 8 for the lower trigram, and the two numbers are added and divided by 6 for the changing line. These are contemporary practitioners' variants as told in one interview. For a classical work on the method she refers to a modern edition printed under Shao Yong's name, and she does not discuss the attribution.
- For the plum-blossom method her text does not say how year, month, day and hour are turned into whole numbers, and it gives no rule for a remainder of zero in the date methods. The third step, the association of figures, she describes as the diviner's skill, quoting Hayek (p. 56). Methods that cast objects (stalks, rice) do not start from a date.
- Andrea Bréard, "Divination with Hexagrams as Combinatorial Practice" (IKGF Occasional Papers, reading session of 27 February 2012), pp. 1 and 18, writes that knowledge of the sixty-four hexagrams, in particular the changing of lines, was the paradigmatic model in the few mathematical sources she studied, independently of cosmology. In Wang Lai's treatise (completed 1799) the one application taken from divination is a hexagram cast with yarrow stalks, and the number of possible changes of one to six lines is computed as 63 (pp. 16-17). Chen Houyao, at the end of the seventeenth century, counts the hexagrams by doubling and as 8 times 8 (p. 5). Jiao Xun uses the arithmetic triangle as a generator of a new order of the hexagrams (p. 7); Bréard does not state the criterion of that order. She says nothing on Shao Yong, Jing Fang, Meng Xi or gua qi.
- Lackner writes (2011, p. 5) that Chinese divination stands much more clearly on the side of reckoning, and hence on the computation of fate.
- Ludwig (2014), pp. 3 and 5-9, describes the geomantic figure as the result of a random act of dots followed by fixed arithmetic, and writes that geomancy made it possible to avoid calculating the exact constellations of the stars. Gerhard Mayer's lecture abstract (fate no. 2, 01|11, p. 8) separates natal-chart methods (solar and lunar returns, transits, directions) from moment-based methods (horary), and says that the first allow a fairly direct link to scientifically recognised models while the second can only be construed as synchronistic and magical. Chardonnens's abstract (fate no. 1, 06|10, p. 5, in German) says that in a scholarly context medieval European divination was almost wholly restricted to the observation of times or to calculations, with a preference for material given as tables or diagrams.

What the project does not take from it: any date-to-number rule or remainder rule, because Homola's text gives no way of turning a date into integers and no rule for a remainder of zero, and the project's chart is computed from its own astronomical base and is kept apart from the step of association that she assigns to the diviner.

## 9. The Yijing

- Smith (2011), pp. 24-26, summarises the Great Commentary's rules for reading a hexagram: the judgment speaks to the image of the whole hexagram and the line statements to the states of change; ji and xiong register success and failure, hui and lin small faults; the first lines are difficult to understand and the top lines easy; the second line usually concerns honour, the fourth fear, the third misfortune and the fifth achievement. He quotes Wang Fuzhi that the aim is not good and bad as such but what to be concerned about and to choose. On pp. 52-53 he gives Zhu Xi's rule for reading a cast by the number of changing lines and adds that there never was, and could not be, general agreement: some diviners require three old lines before a hexagram counts as changed, some always read the derived hexagram, and one late-Qing diviner always read the fifth line.
- Smith (2011), pp. 34-36, dates Jing Fang to 77-37 BCE and calls him a divination specialist credited with calendrical schemes that tie yin-yang, the five phases, trigrams and hexagrams to the stems and branches, the 24 segments of the solar year and the 28 lunar lodges; on p. 51 Zhu Xi is said to have used Jing Fang's Eight Palaces. Smith does not name gua qi, six days seven parts, Meng Xi or the sovereign hexagrams.
- Smith (2011), pp. 45-50 and 57-58, describes the four Song diagrams (Hetu, Luoshu, the Former Heaven chart attributed to Fuxi and the Later Heaven chart attributed to King Wen), says many diagrams came to be attributed to Chen Tuan, presents Shao Yong's *Huangji jingshi* as a system in which numbers produce images, images objects, and notes that Huang Zongxi attacked it and rejected the Former Heaven arrangements as false. He does not say that Shao drew the circle of sixty-four hexagrams.
- Smith (2001), pp. 15-16, describes the Fu Xi arrangement of the sixty-four hexagrams, also called the Former Heaven sequence, as Shao Yong's: a square of 64 hexagrams enclosed by a circle in another order, used as a correlative system in which numbers tied to the hexagrams express non-numerical ideas. He states (p. 16) that in the circle the hexagrams with a yin bottom line are on one side and those with a yang bottom line on the other. The project infers that its Fu Xi sequence has that property: the first half, Fu to Qian, has a yang bottom line and the second half, Gou to Kun, a yin one. Smith reports the attribution; he does not test it.
- Durand-Dastès (2010), p. 8 and note 14, says, citing Alain Arrault, that Li Zhicai and Mu Xiu were involved in passing the Wuji tu and the Xiantian tu from Chen Tuan to Shao Yong. It is second-hand and says nothing of the shape of the diagram.
- Lackner writes (2011, p. 18) that the *Meihua yishu* is a work "unjustifiably" ascribed to the philosopher Shao Yong, and gives no argument.
- The report on the Shao Yong workshop of 20-21 January 2012 (fate no. 5, 05|12, p. 22, by Martin Doesch) says that Arrault presented the phonetic tables of the *Huangji jingshi*, that Bol read the twelve inner chapters numerologically, the number twelve matching the twelve Earthly Branches, and that Chu said Shao Yong tells the future from a systematised past. The sentence "A saint knows heaven and earth; he does not need divination" is the round-table report's and is not clearly Bol's. Sophia Katz's abstract (fate no. 2, 01|11, p. 7) calls Shao Yong the creator of a numerological system for calculating future developments, who after completing the *Huangji jingshi* turned mainly to poetry.
- Tze-ki Hon's lecture abstract (fate no. 8, 01|15, p. 15) says the commentaries Tuan, Xiang and Wenyan treat each hexagram as a structure of space and its six lines as symbols of time. The report on his panel of July 2015 (fate no. 10, 04|16, p. 20, by Zhao Lu) says that a cosmological reading took shape in the first century BCE and CE in which the hexagrams were linked to seasonal changes.
- Smith, "The Book of Changes as a Mirror of the Mind: The Evolution of the Zhouyi (周易) in China and Beyond" (IKGF Occasional Papers, conference paper of 10 April 2009), p. 5, shows with the hexagram Gen how many different names and readings one hexagram has had, so that a hexagram has no single fixed meaning. On pp. 7-8 he gives a case from the *Zuozhuan* of 645 BCE in which a diviner reads the judgment, then the trigram images, then applies the image to the outcome. On p. 25 he notes that printed manuals in Tokugawa Japan used the sixty-four hexagrams to predict rice prices; he does not say whether the forecasts were tested.

What the project does not take from it: any rule for the calendar hexagrams, because the works read here either do not treat gua qi or the sovereign hexagrams or mention them only in one note, and the attribution of the circular arrangement to Shao Yong remains reported and not shown.

## 10. Almanacs and day selection

- Lackner writes (2011, pp. 3-4) that hemerological texts of the fourth century BCE flowed together into what became the Chinese almanac, citing Smith (1992). On pp. 38-40 he describes an all-purpose almanac of today as a calendar core with a miscellany around it (yin-yang and the Eight Trigrams, calculation of fate from the Eight Cyclical Signs, feng shui, recipes), and one example whose calendar tables run from 1901 to 2100.
- The text for the launch of the day-book volume (fate no. 14, 12|18, p. 4, by Lüdke and Schumann) says that its editors, Donald Harper and Marc Kalinowski, define hemerology as concerned with the "ideas, practices, and texts in all cultures that relate time as expressed in the calendar to daily fortune". It says day-books are real-world objects, local technical literature used across social levels, and that the volume is the first book-length systematic treatment of the Chinese material.
- Ulrich Lau's results (fate no. 10, 04|16, p. 10; project page at the IKGF) are that the day-book prescriptions were not followed by common people or officials in the criminal cases from Qin times that he compared (nearly 50 dates), that law officers paid no attention to the zodiac animal of the day of a theft, and, on the project page, that there is no clear evidence that hemerological manuals directly influenced legal decisions in the Qin dynasty. The scope is criminal procedure.
- Claudia von Collani's lecture abstract of 15 May 2012 (fate no. 6, 04|13, p. 10) says that Johann Adam Schall von Bell, head of the Bureau of Astronomy, was responsible for the folk calendar, which rested on belief in spirits and the choice of days; that a fellow Jesuit, Gabriel de Magalhães, attacked the "superstitions" of the calendar, among them the year ghost Taisui and the day spirits; and that Schall and Verbiest read the spirits and the day selection figuratively. The dispute lasted about sixteen years and ended in a papal decision of 1664.
- Doris Ruhe, "Zukunftsschau und Alltagsbewältigung. Volkssprachliche Astrologie im französischen Spätmittelalter" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 27 November 2012), p. 15, reports that the French *Livre de Sydrac* carries the so-called ägyptische Tage (Egyptian days, in the project's translation), days on which every undertaking goes wrong, a calendar of avoidance.
- In the introduction to the Liaowang interview, a statement by Lackner (spoken, as quoted by the journalist) gives as examples of how ordinary prediction was that Yan Fu cast hexagrams every week about his health and fortune and that Wu Mi consulted the day-book to choose a date before his daughter's wedding. No date is given for the wedding.

What the project does not take from it: anything for its day officers, its lodge-of-the-day cycle or its nine-star count, because these works place the almanac in its history and do not state rules for those cycles, which stay checked against the sources named in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

## 11. Topics: what the sources ask about

- Kalinowski (2009), p. 6, writes of two destinies for Wang Chong, a vital one (body and length of life) and a social one (honour, wealth). This is Wang Chong's scheme and not the scheme of BaZi.
- The IKGF research outline (p. 9) says that the terms wealth and prosperity are ubiquitous in popular handbooks, but that the relationship between economics and mantic practices, in the West and in China, is not yet sufficiently understood.
- Constance Cook's report of 11 March 2015 (fate no. 9, 09|15, pp. 19-20) says that the bamboo manuscript *Shifa* (about 300 BCE) uses trigrams to diagnose problems of an elite man's life, marriage, illness and career, and that the position of a trigram in its square and the qualities assigned to it depend on the topic the diviner is asked about.
- Ruhe (2012), p. 32, reports that the horoscopes of the French *Livre de Sydrac* list, for each planet in each sign, the person's physique, social role, health, temperament, marital state and life expectancy. It is a thirteenth-century book of a different tradition from Lilly's.

What the project does not take from it: any definition of a topic, because the topic definitions of the forecast rest on its rule files and their sources, and none of these works defines wealth, career, relationships, health, learning or home and travel in the terms of BaZi, Vedic, Western or I Ching rules.

## 12. The European side

- Sparn (2013), p. 8, reports that Luther and Calvin rejected judicial astrology (astrologia judiciaria or divinatrix) as superstitious and magical, and that the rejection did not concern "natural astrology". On pp. 10-11 he reports that Melanchthon defended astrology as a physical influence and a sign of providence, not the cause of happiness or misery, and as an argument against fatalism. On p. 11 he reports that Cardano compared famous horoscopes in a collection of 1538 and examined them on historical experience, and that judgement (ingenium) was the faculty required. On p. 12 he reports a critique, citing Montaigne, that astrology looked like self-deception through selective empirical evidence. On p. 14 he dates the loss of astrology's scientific standing in Lutheran Protestantism to 1650-1680, and on p. 13 gives as the reason that theology and philosophy stayed committed to a physico-theology on the basis of a Ptolemaic cosmology modified by Tycho Brahe. This concerns Lutheran German universities; Sparn says it was not due to Newton, and he does not mention Lilly. On p. 15 he writes that the attempts to reform astrology observable in England were largely unsuccessful. On pp. 16-17 he reports that Trew questioned the zodiac signs as metaphors, the twelve houses, the Trutina Hermetis and the death forecast from directions.
- Sparn (2013), pp. 5, 13 and 15, reports that annual calendars with prognostications (Practica) were a standing product that since Regiomontanus's ephemerides rested on precise astronomical data. Nothing in the text is about profections or solar returns.
- Esposito (2011) writes that the difference between the divinatory tradition and the modern West is a difference between two forms of rationality and not between rational and irrational (p. 12); that divination takes the form of extremely technical knowledge, controlled and ritualised, and limited to a small group (p. 7); that randomness is used as an instrument to reveal the necessity that governs the world (p. 8); that in ancient Mesopotamia and China the methods led to a divinatory arithmetic and algebra (pp. 8-9); that the spread of alphabetic script went hand in hand with the gradual marginalising of divination in the West (p. 10); that modern thought answers the unknowable future with formalised methods of calculating under contingency (p. 13); that scientific rationality asks for impersonality and replicability, which divination, with the observer inside the observed world, does not give (p. 14); and that scientific predictions can set in motion a process that makes them false, as in self-defeating prophecies (p. 16).
- Ludwig (2014), pp. 10-11, 13, 23 and 27, writes that at the court of Augustus of Saxony geomantic figures 1 to 12 could be assigned to the twelve houses of the horoscope, with the zodiac sign and planet of the moment taken from pre-calculated printed lists; that geomancy never replaced administrative measures as a means of decision (p. 23); and that it enabled the inquirer to co-decide his fate (p. 27). Only the Dresden court is documented (p. 28).
- Ruhe (2012), pp. 13-14, reports from the *Speculum astronomiae* the four branches of judicial astrology (general predictions, nativities, elections, interrogations) and a defence, made in justifying the interrogations, that freedom of decision is not impaired but strengthened.
- Fried (2009), p. 11, writes that astrology, received in the West from the eleventh and twelfth centuries, threatened the freedom of decision that mattered for salvation and so had to be given limits on what it could assert, with partial success.
- Juergen Gebhardt, "Power, Politics and the Quest for Prognostication" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 1 July 2010), p. 5, reads Cicero as separating inspired divination from prudent prediction by reason, skill and experience.
- David Juste's lecture abstract (fate no. 3, 06|11, pp. 6-7) reports that of 83 translations from Arabic made by the first generation of translators (1120-1150), 57 concern astrology and 15 astronomy or mathematics, and that Hermann of Carinthia called astrology a branch of mathematics. The report on a conference of September 2012 (fate no. 6, 04|13, p. 30, by Fidora and Bauer) says that Reimund Leicht showed that mantic arts and astrology in the Arabic and late-antique traditions were commonly recognised forms of knowledge, and that Charles Burnett discussed the primacy of astrological over medical prognoses.

What the project takes from it: the project takes from the European works only background on how astrology was defended and criticised on stated grounds, and nothing for Lilly's tables or rules, because none of these works treats Lilly, the Regiomontanus houses, profections or solar returns as the project uses them.

## 13. Western techniques in Chinese books

- Jeffrey Kotyk's lecture abstract of 10 April 2018 (fate no. 16, 12|19, p. 7) gives a chronology: Indian astrology was available in China from the fourth to the seventh century but not widely used until the eighth; horoscopy developed from the ninth to the sixteenth century; and "we note the introduction of Arab astrology, based primarily on the work of Claudius Ptolemy, during the fourteenth century".
- The IKGF project pages of Haohao Zhu, Wen Zhao, Jeffrey Kotyk and David Pankenier (short descriptions by fellows; they state plans and some results of work in progress) say that Zhu plans to look for the Regiomontanus house system and other European methods in Ming and Qing Chinese books, that Wen Zhao says that the *Lingtai jing* (Daozang no. 288) contains rich information on Dorothean astrology, among it planetary strength from dignities, triplicity rulers for periods of life and annual profection, with exaltation degrees given only by lunar stations, and that Pankenier holds that official astral omenology at court stayed virtually impervious to Indian and Babylonian astrology.
- Lackner writes (2020, fate no. 17, p. 4) that there are only a few traces of Ptolemaic astrology in China.

What the project does not take from it: any claim about the transfer of Western techniques into Chinese practice, because these are short descriptions by fellows and one abstract, and the project's Western rules rest on Lilly, Bonatti and Valens as named in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

## 14. Diagrams (tu)

- Lackner, "Les diagrammes d'analyse textuelle: une pratique savante de la tradition chinoise", in *Les mains de l'intellect* (Lieux de savoir, vol. 2; Paris: Albin Michel, 2011), pp. 824-844, writes (p. 824) that the Chinese notion of tu is a kind of umbrella term that includes schemes, tables, plans, topographical maps and figures. He writes (p. 825) that diagrams are not mere vehicles of a content: they act on it through their figurative character. He writes (p. 836) that cosmological diagrams are usually accompanied by an explanation (tushuo). He describes (p. 837) a diagram of Chen Zhensheng that combines the characters of the hexagrams and their lines with seasons and virtues, and a diagram of the Korean Yi Hwang with the twelve Earthly Branches and four of the five phases tied to the virtues of the first two hexagrams. He writes (p. 838) that the tradition of philological diagrams was not continued under the Ming and that one can only speculate about the causes.
- Smith (2001), p. 40, reports Bouvet's statement of the order of argument: the principles generate the numbers, the numbers the images and the images the charts.
- Lionel Jensen, "Subversive Mythology" (IKGF Occasional Papers, lecture of 3 May 2011), p. 4 note 3, holds that Zhu Xi's diagrams are not mere teaching aids: "One does not read a tu, one apprehends it".

What the project takes from it: only the use of tu as an umbrella word for schemes, tables, plans, maps and figures, and it does not cite the chapter for anything on Shao Yong, the Fu Xi circle, the *Huangji jingshi* or a hexagram wheel, which the chapter does not treat.

## What these readings do not settle

- No BaZi convention is confirmed or contradicted. None of the works read gives a rule for the Zi hour, the turn of the year at Lichun, the day boundary, the luck pillars, the hidden stems, the ten gods or the nayin.
- No topic definition is confirmed or contradicted. The two destinies of Wang Chong and the wish for wealth in popular handbooks are statements about Wang Chong and about handbooks.
- No rule of this project is confirmed or contradicted. Nothing read checks any rule in the four rule files.
- No accuracy of any technique is shown. None of the works tests whether forecasts of any tradition came true.
- The attribution of the Fu Xi circle to Shao Yong is reported by Smith and Durand-Dastès and is not shown. Lackner's remark on the *Meihua yishu* is a caution about one attribution.
- Whether the *Mingpu* has 62 or 64 biographies is not settled here, since Lackner gives 64 in print (2011, p. 7) and 62 in a spoken interview.
- Smith (2009), p. 13 note 35, reporting an entry of Bent Nielsen's *Companion*, places the hexagram Gen among the "Feudal Lords" of the gua qi system in the tenth month. In the project's gua qi sequence the same hexagram stands as a marquis (hou) in the ninth month of the usual list. The rank agrees and the month number does not. Nielsen's text has not been read and the way of counting months has not been checked, so the project changes nothing.
- Fried (2009), p. 11, writes without a source that China knew no planetary astrology and no fate prescribed by the cosmos. This conflicts with Smith (2001), p. 54, who reports Jiang Yong's derivation of the seven luminaries in astrology from the Hetu and Luoshu. It is left unresolved.
- Practitioners disagreed on how to read a cast hexagram (Smith 2011, pp. 52-53). The project does not cast hexagrams and chooses no reading.
