# Sources and accuracy

This page says where each claim in the project comes from and how far that source was checked. Three levels are used throughout. A source is *read* when its text was opened and read; the bibliography then says whether it was *read in full* or *read in part*. It is a *record only* when the work was confirmed to exist in a catalogue or a Crossref entry but its text was not seen. It is *unchecked* when the code or a document names it from general knowledge and its text was not opened. Where a claim could not be checked against the book, this page says "not yet checked against the book" and keeps the citation, so that nothing has been dropped silently. The bibliography near the end sorts every source into one of the three levels.

## Astronomy

Positions of the Sun, Moon and planets, sidereal time and rise and set times come from [astronomy-engine](https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy) 2.1.19 by Don Cross (MIT), which is built on truncated VSOP87 series and the NOVAS reductions and documents itself as accurate to within one arcminute. Longitudes are apparent, geocentric and referred to the true equinox of date. The obliquity is the IAU 2006 mean obliquity. The lunar node is the mean node, a series that is familiar from Jean Meeus, *Astronomical Algorithms*, second edition (1998), chapter 47. The book was not opened for this project: the chapter number is the second edition's, the first edition numbers it differently, and one open implementation prints 476441 for the cubic divisor where this project has 467441. The effect is about three hundred-millionths of a degree, so nothing depends on it, but the divisor should be read from the book before it is relied on.

Swiss Ephemeris is deliberately not used, although it is more accurate, so that the project depends only on permissively licensed code. Its documentation, which is published on the web, was read for the Lahiri ayanamsa; no code, data or text from it is copied, and the few published values quoted in the tests are attributed.

### Known limits

Delta T (the difference between uniform time and the Earth's rotation) is modelled and grows uncertain away from the present: tens of seconds a few centuries back, minutes to hours in antiquity. Nothing here should be trusted to the minute before about 1600.

Zone offsets and daylight saving come from the IANA tz database as shipped in the JavaScript runtime. Its pre-1970 data is acknowledged by its maintainers to be unreliable in places, and in 2014 it merged China's five historical zones into two, Asia/Shanghai and Asia/Urumqi, so a pre-1949 birth in western China is given the history of one of those two unless you supply a fixed offset. The hour at which China's 1986 to 1991 daylight saving switched is itself disputed. When in doubt, give a fixed offset such as `+08:00` and choose the clock basis explicitly.

The "standard time" clock removes daylight saving by taking the smallest of the zone's offsets in January, in July and at the instant. A zone that changed its standard offset in the middle of that year defeats this.

The Lahiri ayanamsa is the true ayanamsa, nutation included, anchored on 23°15′00.658″ at 21 March 1956, 0h Ephemeris Time, and carried to other dates by the IAU 2006 general precession. What is defined and what is derived is set out below, in the Vedic section. Two things limit it: the Indian Astronomical Ephemeris uses a precession rate about 0.3 arcsecond per century larger, so the two part by about 0.2 arcsecond in 2020, and whether the anchor value is a true or a mean one is not settled by any source read, which if it were mean would move every sidereal longitude by about sixteen arcseconds. The nutation series was typed from memory of Meeus's table 22.A and is checked only through its agreement with the published true values above.

## Chinese calendar and BaZi

The sexagenary day count, the Lichun year and the jie months are computed here from the Julian day number and the Sun's longitude and cross-checked against [tyme4ts](https://github.com/6tail/tyme4ts) by 6tail (MIT), which is a development dependency only. The luck-pillar start age uses the rule of three days to the year carried to the second, the default of that library. Hidden stems, ten gods and nayin follow the common modern tables; the nayin are checked against the same library. That comparison shows the tables agree with a widely used implementation, not that they agree with any text: no classical or scholarly source was read for them.

The twelve day officers, the twenty-eight lodge days and the nine-star year are checked against the same library over four hundred days. Ho (2003, p. 30, a folio number from a scan) says the day-officer cycle restarts at each of the twelve jie, which is the rule the project uses for its month-branch logic.

Historical note: the solar terms have been defined by the true Sun since the Shixian calendar of 1645. Before that they were equal divisions of the year in time (pingqi). This version always uses the true-Sun rule. Aslaksen writes that "it was not until the last calendar reform in 1645 that they started using the true Sun", and Liu writes: "Before 1645, the 24 solar terms were calculated by dividing the tropical year evenly into 24 periods." The sources are Helmer Aslaksen, "The Mathematics of the Chinese Calendar", an unpublished paper from the National University of Singapore, of which the copy read is dated 13 May 2004, and Y. T. Liu, "24 Solar Terms (二十四節氣)", last major update February 2019.

The history of BaZi (the move from three pillars to four, and who first used which) is not stated anywhere in the project as sourced, and no work read for the project settles it. Lackner writes (IKGF Occasional Paper, 2011, p. 4) that the attribution of qualities to points in time led, by the twelfth century in Western reckoning, to the Chinese horoscope called bazi suanming, and he names no source for the date. In a spoken interview (The Paper, 12 October 2014) he says that fate calculation began to sprout in the Tang, spread in the Song, and that the Ziping method appeared in the Song, again without a source. In a spoken lecture (13 June 2017, at 0:47) he says that the calculation of destiny developed out of hemerology, on the basis of the qualities of a particular date. He calls Wan Minying's *Sanming tonghui*, of about 1550, the standard work for calculating destiny from the Eight Cyclical Signs, which appeared in 2009 in an illustrated edition (2011, p. 24), and he describes Yuan Shushan's *Mingpu* as biographies of 64 famous Chinese (2011, p. 7); in a spoken interview with Luo Ying he gives 62. These are a scholar's summary statements and leads to primary texts, and no convention of the project rests on any of them. That the Zi double-hour begins at 23:00 is the convention the project names `zi23`; the Needham page given for it (about p. 312) is possibly off by one and has not been rechecked, so it is not cited.

## I Ching

The hexagram table is structure only: King Wen number, Chinese name, pinyin and the six lines. The English glosses are this project's own short literal renderings. No translation of the text is included; James Legge's is in the public domain, Wilhelm and Baynes's is not.

The classical text of the *Zhouyi* is in the public domain. The transcription of its characters in the code was taken from Chinese Wikisource, the page 周易 (<https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/周易>) and its sub-pages 周易/<hexagram name>. Wikisource offers its text under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 licence (CC BY-SA 4.0). If any right subsists in the transcription, it is used under that licence, with this credit.

The gua qi scheme implemented is Meng Xi's, as Yi Xing adopted it in the Dayan calendar: sixty hexagrams of six days seven parts each from Zhong Fu at the winter solstice. Yi Xing argues for it in the essay Gua yi in *Xin Tang shu* (New Tang History), juan 27A (新唐書/卷027上), with the words 當據孟氏，自冬至初，中孚用事, and the same essay reports a different allotment of Jing Fang's, which the project offers as the `jing-fang` convention (Kan, Li, Zhen and Dui at 73/80 of a day, Yi, Jin, Jing and Da Xu at 5 days 14 parts, the rest at 6 days 7 parts). Yi Xing calls the 73-part allotment unorthodox (其說不經), and the project implements it for comparison, not as a recommendation. The *Yi wei Jilantu* is cited only for beginning the count at Zhong Fu (Sun Jiran, 早期卦氣思想溯源, 中國文化研究所學報 69, July 2019, p. 1). Neither the Jilantu itself nor a passage in it assigning the twelve hexagrams to months was seen.

The gua qi sequence and the assignment of the four cardinal hexagrams' lines to the solar terms were verified against the table of the Dayan calendar in *Xin Tang shu*, juan 28A, in the transcription at zh.wikisource.org (新唐書/卷028上). The two agree at all sixty positions but one, where the transcription has 史 for 夬; 夬 is the sovereign hexagram of the third month and nothing else can stand there. A second slip stands in the Bailu row, where the transcription has 候 for 侯 (marquis) beside 巽; it changes no hexagram. The same table shows that each month's duke hexagram begins at the zhongqi and that the marquis hexagram straddles the following jie, which is the arrangement implemented. It also places each month's sovereign hexagram inside the span from zhongqi to zhongqi, not the branch month from jie to jie; the default ring and track draw the sovereigns from the jie, and the `sovereignBoundary` convention moves them to the zhongqi. Yi Xing says the twelve month hexagrams come from Meng Xi's Zhangju (章句, 27A). The English ranks (Duke, Sovereign, Marquis, Great Officer, Minister; gong, bi, hou, daifu, qing) and "cardinal hexagrams" (sizheng gua) are this project's own renderings, for which no scholarly translation was found.

The Fu Xi circular arrangement, xiantian (Former Heaven), is attributed to Shao Yong; it is generated here from its binary rule. The attribution is the conventional one, and it has been reported and not shown. Smith (IKGF Occasional Paper, 2001, pp. 15-16) describes the arrangement as Shao Yong's: a square of the sixty-four hexagrams enclosed by a circle in another order, used as a correlative system in which numbers tied to the hexagrams express non-numerical ideas. He states one property of the circle, that the hexagrams with a yin bottom line lie on one side and those with a yang bottom line on the other, and the project's sequence has that property (the first half, Fu to Qian, has a yang bottom line and the second half, Gou to Kun, a yin one). Durand-Dastès (IKGF Occasional Paper, 2010, p. 8, citing Arrault) names the xiantian diagram in a line of transmission from Chen Tuan to Shao Yong, at second hand. Lackner (2011, p. 18) writes that the *Meihua yishu* is "unjustifiably" ascribed to Shao Yong and gives no argument, so the project does not credit the plum-blossom method to Shao Yong. Lackner says in a spoken lecture (13 June 2017, at 1:05) that the *Meihua yishu* is a form of oracle attributed to Shao Yong that came to light much later, about the sixteenth century. "Former Heaven" is the gloss that Smith uses (2001; 2011, pp. 45-50) and the project's own English label. The usage of the Brill *Handbook* has not been checked, because the Handbook has not been read. The sixty-four-gate wheel is the arrangement used by twentieth-century systems that set the hexagrams on the tropical zodiac; its order and starting point are facts about those systems, reproduced without their interpretive material. For background on all of these see Bent Nielsen, *A Companion to Yi jing Numerology and Cosmology* (2003), whose entries on xiao xi gua and gua qi are not yet checked against the book.

## Western

Domiciles, exaltations and Chaldean faces are the standard tables, stated from general knowledge and not checked against a text. Triplicity lords are those attributed to Dorotheus of Sidon, whose *Carmen Astrologicum* is edited by Pingree (Leipzig: Teubner, 1976); the edition was not opened, and only the Fire and Earth rows were corroborated, in secondary snippets, so the Air and Water rows are not yet checked against the book. Bounds are the Egyptian bounds. Ptolemy lists them in *Tetrabiblos* I.20 in the Robbins (Loeb, 1940) numbering, which is I.21 in the Boll-Boer and Hübner numbering, and then prefers a system of his own; the numbering rests on tables of contents seen only in a table of contents, and the text of the bounds was not read. That Dorotheus and Valens also used the Egyptian bounds is not yet checked against the books. The test suite checks that the bounds sum to the planets' greater years (Saturn 57, Jupiter 79, Mars 66, Venus 82, Mercury 76); that is arithmetic on the project's own table, which was also compared with the familiar Egyptian bounds from memory, not with a primary text. Annual profections follow Valens, *Anthology* IV, whose chapter and edition are still to be supplied and which is not yet checked against the book; that a profection turns at the tropical solar return is a modern convention, and what Valens himself does was not established. Firdaria follow the medieval scheme of nine periods totalling seventy-five years with sub-periods in Chaldean order, and the two orderings of the nodes for night charts are both offered; the structure comes from a secondary summary of Abu Ma'shar and Shahmardan, neither of whom was read. The Ptolemaic bounds and triplicities that William Lilly printed in *Christian Astrology* (1647) are not yet in the engine's drawings. For the forecast rules, Lilly's book was opened and its table of dignities was compared cell by cell with the page; see the forecast section. Houlding's article reproduces Lilly's terms from p. 104 of the 1647 edition and notes that they differ from Ptolemy's in Gemini, where Saturn and Mars are reversed, so a choice between Lilly's table and the Robbins or Hübner table has to be made when they are added; Lilly's table was not opened.

## Vedic

The Lahiri (Chitrapaksha) ayanamsa was rebuilt on its definition. The Calendar Reform Committee of 1955 set the start of the religious solar year at a tropical Sun longitude of 23°15′, and the Swiss Ephemeris documentation states the definition as 23°15′00″ on 21 March 1956, 0h Ephemeris Time, and reports that the Indian Astronomical Ephemeris has used 23°15′00.658″ since 1985; the project uses the revised value. The value at any other date is derived by carrying that anchor along the IAU 2006 general precession and adding the nutation in longitude, which is the true ayanamsa. What is not established is whether the anchor is itself a true or a mean value: the Ephemeris's own footnote calls it mean, another page gives exactly 23°15′00″, and the Swiss Ephemeris authors conclude from the Ephemeris's true values for 2019 that it is true. The project follows them, and states the consequence of the other reading, about sixteen arcseconds.

A constant of 23°51′11″ at J2000 would be unsourced; it is 14 arcseconds below the Ephemeris's mean value at J2000 (23°51′25.53″ as quoted by the Swiss Ephemeris documentation) and about 30 arcseconds below the Committee's definition when run back to 1956, so it is not used. A reading of p. 251 of the CSIR reprint of Part C of the Committee's report (Saha and Lahiri, *History of the Calendar*), which puts the start of the religious solar year at a tropical Sun longitude of 23°15′, was made once and was not confirmed independently; the report is otherwise not yet checked against the book, and the Ephemeris volumes were not seen, only the figures quoted from them. The zero point, run backward, falls in about 285 CE, which agrees with the zero point of 285 CE that the Swiss Ephemeris documentation quotes Lahiri as giving.

Nakshatra lords and vimshottari period lengths are the standard ones, attributed to Parashara. The *Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra* is a later compilation under that name; no edition, translation or chapter has been cited or read, so this is not yet checked against the book. Years are taken as 365.25 days, which is a software convention, since vimshottari practice also uses 360-day years. Pingree's *Jyotiḥśāstra* (1981) is cited in the correspondence registry for the nakshatras becoming equal arcs; its existence was confirmed from a catalogue record and its text was not seen.

## Maya

The Long Count, Tzolkin and Haab are computed from the Julian day number with the Goodman-Martinez-Thompson correlation constant 584283 by default. Martin and Skidmore (2012, p. 3) call 584285 and 584283 the two most widely accepted values, say 584285 is Thompson's own value from 1927 onwards, and say 584283 is popular among those who hold that the highland day count is an unbroken tradition. 584283 is therefore a common default and not a settled value. They argue for 584286 from records of the movements of Venus, and the project offers it. No source seen calls 584285 "the astronomical variant", so the project does not use that label. Reingold and Dershowitz, *Calendrical Calculations*, is cited for 584283; the constant was seen in a port of the book's code, not in the book, and which author is named first depends on the edition (see the bibliography), so the citation is not yet checked against the book.

## Day-count cycles of the almanac

The twenty-eight-day cycle of lodges (xiu) fixes the weekday: Jiao always falls on a Thursday and Fang on a Sunday, and a test checks that arithmetic. The pairing of lodges with weekdays follows the Chinese almanac convention, and it was found consistent with a table at 6tail.cn. Its historical source has not been checked. The *Xiuyao jing* (Taishō 1299) is the usual candidate, but it counts twenty-seven lodges and is not cited for the twenty-eight-day count until it has been read.

## Forecast rules

The rules of the forecast are in four files in `packages/core/src/forecast/data/`, and every rule names its source and the place in it. A rule is kept in the files only where someone had the passage in front of them. The sources are listed in the three groups below. This section says in what form each was read, because that decides how far a rule can be trusted. In every tradition the rule's words are the project's own; a quotation is at most 25 words. Where a rule file says that a number, a threshold or a weight is the project's own and not the source's, it says so in the rule.

**BaZi.** The rules rest on 淵海子平, 三命通會 (Wan Minying) and 滴天髓, the last in the 闡微 edition (Ren Tieqiao) and the 輯要 edition. They were read as online texts. The hidden-stems rule names Chinese Wikisource for the opening section of 淵海子平, and the rule on 通關 notes a miscopy in the 滴天髓 text it was read from (金林 for 金木); the files do not name the editions otherwise, so no page numbers are given. The ordering of the judgement (the day master first, then the month, then the luck pillar and the year) follows these books, and the numbers (the strength score and its bands, the weights of luck pillar and year) are modern conventions that no source gives. 窮通寶鑑 was not opened, so the tiaohou method (seasonal adjustment) is not offered. The 子平真詮 route through the month structure is recorded in the method note as a variant and no rule rests on it.

**Vedic.** An English translation was read, and no Sanskrit. For that reason no quotation is shown for any Vedic rule. The *Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra* was read in the English translation at the Internet Archive item `ParasharaHoraSastra`, a text compiled with additions from Santhanam; its title page was not seen, so the translator and publisher are unconfirmed, and the chapter and verse numbers in the rules are that copy's. Its OCR is noisy. The *Phaladeepika* of Mantreswara was read in the translation of V. Subrahmanya Sastri (1937; second edition 1950), Internet Archive item `in.ernet.dli.2015.92117`, and B. V. Raman's *Hindu Predictive Astrology* from the Internet Archive item `hindupredictiveastrologyofbvraman`, whose edition was not named. The strengths of 1 to 3 in the rule file are the project's own reading of emphasis, and the sources give no arithmetic for adding one statement to another.

**Modern translations are locators only.** Where a rule rests on an English translation (the Vedic texts, Riley's Valens), the translation is cited so that a reader can find the passage, page or verse. The sentences the engine prints are its own wording of the rule's facts, and no quotation from a translation is shown; the quotations that are shown are from works in the public domain (Lilly 1647, Bonatti 1550, and the Chinese classical texts) and are at most 25 words.

**Western.** The rules follow William Lilly, *Christian Astrology* (London, 1647), read from page images of the Wellcome Library copy (Internet Archive item `b30338724`, 894 leaves), with the printed page number equal to the leaf number minus 34. Every table the rules use (the essential dignities and their scores, the accidental fortitudes and debilities, the orbs, the profection tables) was compared with the page, cell by cell. Of the 57 rules first drafted, 43 matched the page in substance, 13 were corrected and one was moved out as unconfirmed. Other 1647 scans were used only to cross-check the text layer. Guido Bonatti, *De astronomia tractatus X* (Basel, 1550), was read in Latin from the Internet Archive item `bub_gb_BgWuc5O-JzIC`; the page numbers cited for pp. 817-818 come from OCR running heads only. Vettius Valens, *Anthologies*, was read in the translation of Mark T. Riley (2019), Internet Archive item `vettius-valens-anthologies`, with its text layer; the page numbers are Riley's, printed at the foot of each page, and chapters IV.5, IV.11, IV.14 and V.2 were opened. The thresholds that turn Lilly's words into classes (for instance the score at which a planet counts as strong) are the project's, and the rule says so.

**I Ching.** The *Zhouyi* text and the *Xici zhuan* were read from Chinese Wikisource (周易 and its chapters). The *Yi wei Jilantu* was read in the Siku quanshu text with the commentary of Zheng Xuan at the same site, and the *Xin Tang shu*, juan 27A, which is also in the first group below. The calendar parts are attested in these sources. The grading of the hexagram and line texts by a table of verdict words is a construction of the project; the *Xici zhuan* gives the ordering of the words (gain and loss, small flaw, good at repairing a fault) and the rest is the engine's own convention. The part is marked experimental wherever it is shown. The forecast does not consult the Yijing by casting, so no limit on consultation applies to it. Lackner says in a spoken lecture that the Yijing could be consulted at most three times for the same question (13 June 2017, at 1:00), and that an oracle can be consulted only two or three times at most, but that its content can be misunderstood (13 June 2017, at 0:27).

Rules whose source could not be confirmed are in `held-back.json` with the reason, and are not evaluated: 13 in BaZi, 4 in Vedic, 8 in Western and 4 in the I Ching, each a rule, a table or a single clause.

## English terminology

Most of the English labels in the program are older than this project and follow trade usage. This section records which of them now follow a scholarly source, and which stay the project's own. Each label was checked against the sources it names. Scholarly usage is itself split on several of these, and where it is, the label chosen is a choice of the project, not a settled usage.

### Labels that are trade usage or the project's own and stay unverified

Several labels have no scholarly rendering behind them. The English names of the ten gods other than the two above, such as "Friend" for bijian, are trade usage. Ho gives "shoulder-of-equal-level" as the literal gloss for 比肩, and the alternative trade name "Companion" rests on a practitioner page that nobody opened, so neither is scholarly. The thirty English names of the nayin are the project's popular glosses, and no source read for this project gives an English rendering. The five ranks of the gua qi hexagrams (Duke, Sovereign, Marquis, Great Officer and Minister, which are gong, bi, hou, daifu and qing) and the "cardinal hexagrams" (sizheng gua) are the project's own English, for which no scholarly rendering was found. "Sovereign hexagrams" for bigua, with "waning and waxing (xiaoxi)", is supported only by a draft translation marked not for citation, which was not opened; Nielsen's entry should be read before it is relied on. The wording of the double-hour (shichen) and the Zi double-hour from 23:00 to 01:00 rests on Needham and Ho pages that were not rechecked.

## Translations

The project embeds no translation of any classical text. The hexagram names carry short glosses of the project's own, and nothing from a published translation is reproduced. The Thesaurus Linguae Sericae (TLS), hxwd.org, hosts the Chinese text of the Zhouyi, but no human translation of it was found there. Its footer states that the site is "licensed under a CC BY SA license (except some translations)", and the exception is not explained, so nothing from it is reproduced. No text from the other works in the bibliography is copied either; where a source is quoted, the quotation is brief and attributed, and classical Chinese is quoted only from the transcriptions named above.

## To read next

Several open questions would be settled by sources not yet read. Nielsen's *Companion*, in its entries on xiao xi gua and gua qi, would settle where the English label "sovereign hexagrams" comes from and whether the month assignment and the gua qi scheme have a source earlier than Yi Xing's report, and his chapter on the thirty-two-year hexagram cycle in the weishu literature would show whether that cycle is something the project should offer. Kalinowski's typology in the *Handbook* and Steavu's chapter on the shi board, neither of which has been read for this project, would have to be read before any catalogue of which mantic arts are computable, or any claim about Taiyi, Qimen and Liuren, is written. Homola's chapter in MPIWG Preprint 496, which has been read, shows how some date methods compute, but it leaves open how a date becomes a whole number and what a remainder of zero means, so it does not settle such a catalogue either. Bol and Chu Ping-Tzu in the 2013 *Monumenta Serica* section might say whether the Fu Xi circle is Shao Yong's. Chang's chapter in *Coping with the Future* is the first thing to read before anything is said about the exchange between Chinese and Western astrology. The 2025 open-access chapter of Lackner may bear on how divination is classified and on the question of fate and freedom, but its licence allows citing only. A library copy of Needham vol. 3 would settle the page ranges for the lodges and the Jupiter count, the Calendar Reform Committee's report and the Indian Astronomical Ephemeris would settle whether the Lahiri anchor is a true or a mean value, Meeus would settle the divisor of the node series, and Valens, Dorotheus and the *Xiuyao jing* would settle the profection rule, the Air and Water triplicities and the pairing of lodges with weekdays.

 Hayek's chapter on hand counting in *Coping with the Future* (pp. 530-558), which Homola cites, and the primary texts that Lackner names, Wan Minying's *Sanming tonghui* in its 2009 reissue and Yuan Shushan's *Mingpu*, would take the history of BaZi from a scholar's summary to sources.
